### ringkas-en-001
The seven steps of Managing Problems can be reviewed in one page. Step one lists problems and sorts them into solvable problems, which can be influenced or changed, and unsolvable problems, which cannot. Step two chooses a solvable problem to start with, and an easier one first is recommended so that early success is possible. Step three defines the problem: keep only the elements that are practical and can be controlled or influenced, keep the explanation specific and brief, do not fold two problems into one, and break a problem with many parts into separate parts. Step four brainstorms as many solutions as possible without judging them yet, including what the client can do alone, who could help with parts of the problem, and existing strengths, resources, or support. Step five decides and chooses the helpful strategies: those with very few disadvantages for the client or others, those the client can actually carry out with the means available, and more than one may be chosen. Step six builds an action plan with a detailed sequence, the day and time for each task, the order in which solutions will be tried, the resources needed, and a reminder system such as notes, a calendar, or tasks tied to meals and other routines. Step seven reviews in the next session what was done, what effect it had on the original problem, which difficulties appeared, and what will be continued in the following week.
### ringkas-en-002
A completed worksheet shows what the steps look like in practice. The solvable problem was reducing fights with the husband and improving low mood, while the unsolvable problems were getting the husband a job and stopping his anger. The chosen problem was the daily fighting, defined as reducing the number of arguments, which at that point happened every day. The possible ways of managing the problem included doing nothing and waiting, telling the husband to see a community elder, telling him to try harder to find work, looking for a way to earn money, talking with friends and asking their advice, asking the mother for advice, telling the husband she was not happy, and leaving him. The helpful solutions chosen were talking with a trusted friend who had faced a similar problem, asking the mother for advice briefly, and telling the husband she was not happy. The plan named Tuesday morning to visit the trusted friend after taking the children to school, Thursday to mention the problem to her mother while visiting and ask what she would do, and Saturday morning to tell her husband she had been unhappy because of the fighting and then wait for his response. The review recorded that the friend was supportive and the mood improved a little, that the mother helped her rehearse the conversation, that the husband agreed but the couple still did not know what to do, and that two big fights occurred instead of daily ones.
