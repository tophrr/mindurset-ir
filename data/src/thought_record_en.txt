### tr-en-001
A thought record is a written exercise for examining a thought that has just made an emotion stronger. It is not a diary of events and it is not positive thinking. The purpose is narrow, namely to test one specific thought against what is actually known, so that the emotion attached to it can be reviewed instead of obeyed. Use it when an emotion rises quickly, when the same interpretation keeps returning, or when a thought feels like a fact even though no evidence has been checked. Do not use it in the middle of a crisis, during a panic surge, or while a person is too exhausted to write. In those moments the shorter skills come first, because a seven-column exercise asks for attention that a crisis takes away. One record examines one thought, and a useful record takes ten to twenty minutes, not an hour.
### tr-en-002
The record has seven columns, and each column answers one question. Column one asks what happened, in concrete terms, including when and where. Column two asks which emotion came up and how strong it was on a scale from zero to one hundred. Column three asks what went through the mind at that moment, written as a sentence in the first person. Column four asks what evidence supports that sentence: facts, not feelings, not other people's guesses. Column five asks what evidence does not fit the sentence, including what a careful observer would notice. Column six asks for a balanced sentence that survives both columns of evidence. Column seven asks how strong the emotion is after the balanced sentence, and what the person will do next.
### tr-en-003
The first two columns are the easiest to hurry and the most important to get right. In column one, write the situation as a camera would record it: the exam was handed back with a mark of fifty-eight, the message arrived at eleven at night, the meeting was moved without notice. Avoid words that already contain an interpretation, such as humiliating, unfair, or proof. In column two, name the emotion in one or two words, for example anxious, ashamed, angry, sad, and then rate its strength out of one hundred. The rating is not a measurement and it does not need to be consistent across days. Its only job is to give the later columns something to compare against, so the same person can see whether the exercise changed anything at all.
### tr-en-004
Columns three to five carry the work. In column three, write the automatic thought exactly as it arrived, without softening it: I am going to fail this degree, everyone saw that I am not good enough, there is no point trying again. In column four, list the facts that support the thought and nothing else, which usually produces a shorter list than expected. In column five, list what contradicts the thought: occasions when the same prediction did not come true, skills that were used, people who responded differently, information that was missing on the day. A thought is not examined by arguing with it, only by placing the evidence for it next to the evidence against it and letting the two lists stand side by side.
### tr-en-005
Column six asks for a sentence that both lists can live with. It is usually longer than the original thought, less certain, and closer to what an outside observer would say: this mark says I did badly on one exam, it does not say how the whole degree ends, and I have recovered from a bad mark before. A balanced sentence that simply contradicts the first thought, such as I am actually doing fine, is not balanced and will not be believed. In column seven, rate the emotion again and note any change, even a small one, then write one action that follows from the new sentence: ask the lecturer which topics to repeat, tell a friend what happened, plan the next study block.
### tr-en-006
A worked example shows how the columns connect. A student receives a group project mark of sixty-two out of a hundred, lower than expected, and the first thought is that the group carried the student and that everyone now knows it. Column one records the mark being posted in the shared document on a Wednesday afternoon. Column two records shame at eighty and anxiety at seventy. Column three records the thought in full, including the part about being found out. Column four records the facts that support it: the student wrote fewer sections than two other members and missed one meeting. Column five records the facts against it: the student built the analysis everyone used, ran the final check before submission, and the tutor's written comment names that analysis as the strongest part.
### tr-en-007
In the same example, column six produces a sentence that fits the evidence: my written contribution was smaller than two members' this time, the analysis I built was used by the whole group, and one mark does not decide what I can do next semester. Column seven records shame dropping from eighty to fifty-five and anxiety from seventy to forty-five, followed by two actions: ask the tutor which sections were weakest, and take a larger writing role in the next project with an earlier start. The record does not make the mark better and does not remove the disappointment. What it changes is the conclusion drawn from the mark, and that conclusion is what kept the emotion running.
### tr-en-008
Several difficulties appear again and again. The first is confusing a thought with an emotion: I feel like a failure is a thought, while shame is the emotion, and they belong in different columns. The second is writing interpretations in column one, which hides the thought that needs testing. The third is filling column five with reassurance rather than evidence, which produces a sentence the person cannot believe. The fourth is expecting the emotion to fall to zero, when a drop of fifteen or twenty points is the ordinary result. The fifth is doing a record once and judging the whole method by it. A record is a skill, and like any skill it is clumsy for the first few attempts and quick only after repetition.
### tr-en-009
The record also helps name the habits that keep a thought in place. All-or-nothing thinking treats a result as either complete success or complete failure. Catastrophising jumps from one event to the worst possible ending. Mind reading assumes what others are thinking without asking them. Fortune telling treats a prediction as a fact that has already happened. Overgeneralisation turns one bad evening into always and never. Personalisation makes the person responsible for events they did not control. Should statements turn preferences into obligations that produce guilt. Mental filtering keeps the negative detail and drops the rest. Emotional reasoning treats a feeling as proof. Labelling replaces a description of behaviour with a verdict on the whole person. Naming the habit is not the same as answering it, but the name makes the automatic thought easier to see and easier to test in columns four and five.
